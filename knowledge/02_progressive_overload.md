# Progressive Overload

## Purpose

Progressive overload means gradually increasing the training challenge
when the body has adapted sufficiently to the current workload.

It is one of the main mechanisms used to continue making progress over
time.

Progressive overload does NOT mean increasing the workload every workout
or every week.

The appropriate progression depends on the user's performance, recovery,
and current training goal.

---

# 1. Why Progressive Overload Matters

If the training stimulus remains unchanged indefinitely, the body may
eventually adapt to it.

Once a workload becomes sufficiently easy, additional challenge may be
required to continue progressing.

The agent should therefore look for opportunities to increase training
stimulus when appropriate.

However, progression must be balanced against recovery.

---

# 2. Ways to Progress

Progression can occur through several variables.

### Resistance

Increase the external load.

Examples:

- Heavier backpack
- Stronger resistance band
- More band tension
- Additional weight

This is often the clearest progression method when equipment allows it.

### Repetitions

Increase repetitions while maintaining appropriate technique and
proximity to failure.

Example:

10 → 11 → 12 → 13 → 14

Repetitions should only count as progression when technique and target
RIR remain reasonably consistent.

### Sets

Add additional working sets.

Example:

2 sets → 3 sets

Adding sets increases total training volume and therefore also increases
fatigue.

It should not be the default progression method when simpler options
are available.

### Exercise Difficulty

Move to a harder variation.

Examples:

Incline push-up
→ Standard push-up
→ Band-resisted push-up

or:

Assisted chin-up
→ Less assistance
→ Bodyweight chin-up
→ Weighted chin-up

This is particularly important for bodyweight training.

### Range of Motion

Progress by gradually increasing useful range of motion when the user
can control it.

Range of motion should not be increased if doing so creates pain or
substantially degrades technique.

### Execution Quality

Improving technique can itself represent meaningful progression.

Examples:

- Better control
- More consistent tempo
- Better stability
- Full intended range of motion
- Less compensatory movement

This is especially useful when an exercise is still being learned.

---

# 3. Double Progression

Double progression is a useful default method for many exercises.

The user works within a repetition range.

Example:

3 × 8–15 @ ~2 RIR

The user gradually increases repetitions while keeping approximately the
same resistance and effort.

Example:

Session 1: 10 / 9 / 8
Session 2: 11 / 10 / 9
Session 3: 12 / 11 / 10
...
Session X: 15 / 15 / 15

Once the user can consistently reach the upper end of the target range
with appropriate technique and RIR, resistance or exercise difficulty
can be increased.

After increasing difficulty, repetitions will usually fall again.

Example:

15 / 15 / 15
↓ increase resistance
10 / 9 / 8
↓
11 / 10 / 9
↓
...

This creates another progression cycle.

---

# 4. RIR-Based Progression

Progression must account for effort.

A user performing:

12 reps @ 4 RIR

is not equivalent to:

12 reps @ 1 RIR

The second set represents a substantially greater training demand.

Therefore, the agent should evaluate:

- Repetitions
- Resistance
- Technique
- RIR

rather than repetitions alone.

---

# 5. When to Progress

The agent should consider progression when:

- The user completes the intended rep range.
- Technique remains acceptable.
- Target RIR is approximately achieved.
- Performance is stable or improving.
- Recovery is adequate.
- No significant pain is present.

Progression should be gradual.

Do not increase several stress variables simultaneously unless there is
a specific reason.

---

# 6. When NOT to Progress

Do not automatically increase difficulty when:

- Recovery is poor.
- Performance is declining.
- The user is unusually fatigued.
- Technique is deteriorating.
- Joint or tendon pain is increasing.
- Running fatigue is interfering with lower-body performance.
- Sleep has been consistently poor.
- The user is under-recovered from recent training.

Maintaining the current workload can be the correct decision.

---

# 7. Progression Is Not Linear

Progress rarely follows a perfect upward line.

A realistic pattern may look like:

Progress
Progress
Progress
Stable
Progress
Small regression
Progress

Short-term fluctuations are normal.

The agent should evaluate trends rather than reacting to every single
session.

One bad workout should not automatically trigger a program change.

---

# 8. Plateaus

A plateau occurs when meaningful progress stops for a sustained period,
not merely after one difficult session.

Before changing the program, investigate:

- Sleep
- Nutrition
- Recovery
- Training consistency
- Technique
- RIR accuracy
- Exercise selection
- Total volume
- Running fatigue
- Stress
- Equipment limitations

Only after identifying the likely bottleneck should the agent modify
training.

---

# 9. Plateau Decision Process

If progression stalls:

Check consistency
↓
Check recovery
↓
Check nutrition
↓
Check technique
↓
Check actual effort/RIR
↓
Check total training load
↓
Check running interference
↓
Modify training if necessary

Do not immediately add more sets.

More training is not always the solution to a plateau.

---

# 10. Bodyweight Progression

Bodyweight exercises require special consideration because resistance
cannot always be adjusted in small increments.

Possible progression methods:

1. Improve technique.
2. Increase repetitions.
3. Increase range of motion.
4. Add external resistance.
5. Use a harder variation.
6. Reduce assistance.
7. Increase sets when appropriate.

The agent should avoid endlessly increasing repetitions when the goal
would be better served by increasing exercise difficulty.

---

# 11. Band Training

Resistance bands can be difficult to quantify precisely because actual
resistance varies with:

- Band strength
- Stretch length
- Anchor position
- Body position
- Range of motion

Therefore, the agent should not treat band labels as perfectly precise
loads.

Useful tracking variables include:

- Band used
- Anchor/setup
- Repetitions
- RIR
- Range of motion
- Exercise variation

Consistency of setup is important for comparing sessions.

---

# 12. Backpack Loading

A loadable backpack can provide progressive external resistance.

When using a backpack:

- Record approximate load.
- Keep the setup consistent.
- Ensure the load is secure.
- Avoid excessive loading that changes technique.
- Increase load gradually.

Backpack load should be treated as approximate rather than perfectly
calibrated gym-equipment resistance.

---

# 13. Progression Across Different Exercises

Not every exercise needs to progress at the same rate.

For example:

- Push-up: progressing
- Band row: progressing
- Split squat: stable
- Calf raise: progressing

This is normal.

The agent should evaluate each movement independently while considering
overall fatigue.

Do not force progression on a lagging movement by sacrificing technique.

---

# 14. Lower-Body Progression With Running

Lower-body strength progression must account for running load.

For example, if running volume or intensity has recently increased,
lower-body resistance training may temporarily progress more slowly.

The agent should consider total lower-body stress:

Strength stress
+
Running stress
+
Recovery capacity

before increasing lower-body training.

---

# 15. Progression and Training Phases

Progression rules can change depending on the current phase.

During a strength-focused phase:

- Resistance progression may receive greater emphasis.
- Running may be maintained at manageable levels.

During an endurance-focused phase:

- Running progression may receive greater emphasis.
- Strength may shift toward maintenance.

During a recovery phase:

- Progression may temporarily stop.
- The goal becomes restoring performance and reducing fatigue.

The agent should always know the current phase before making major
progression decisions.

---

# 16. Minimum Effective Progression

Progression does not need to be dramatic.

Small improvements are valid:

- One additional repetition
- Slightly better technique
- Slightly more resistance
- One additional controlled set
- Reduced assistance
- Improved range of motion

The agent should recognize small improvements instead of demanding large
jumps.

---

# 17. Avoiding Junk Progression

A numerical increase is not automatically meaningful progression.

Examples of fake progression:

- More repetitions achieved with substantially worse technique.
- More weight achieved through compensatory movement.
- Shortened range of motion to increase reps.
- Increasing workload while RIR becomes much higher.
- Increasing training volume while recovery collapses.

Progress should represent a genuine increase in useful training stimulus.

---

# 18. Progression Decision Rules

If the user reaches the top of the rep range + target RIR:

Consider increasing resistance or exercise difficulty.

If the user reaches the top of the rep range but is much farther
from failure than expected:

Increase difficulty rather than simply adding more repetitions.

If the user is below the target range:

Maintain the current difficulty and work toward the target range.

If performance is improving but recovery is worsening:

Do not automatically progress.

Consider maintaining or reducing training stress.

If performance is declining repeatedly:

Investigate recovery and total training load before progressing.

If technique is poor:

Reduce difficulty until technique is acceptable.

If pain is increasing:

Do not use progression to push through it.

Modify or stop the movement and assess appropriately.

---

# 19. Tracking Progression

For each resistance exercise, the system should ideally store:

- Exercise
- Variation
- Resistance/load
- Sets
- Repetitions
- RIR
- Range of motion when relevant
- Technique notes
- Pain/discomfort
- Date

This allows the agent to compare the current session with previous
performances.

---

# 20. Core Principle

Progressive overload is not:

"Do more every workout."

It is:

Gradually increase the useful training stimulus when the current
workload has been sufficiently adapted to and the user can recover from
the additional demand.
