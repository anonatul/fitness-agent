# Volume, Frequency, and Intensity

## Purpose

Volume, frequency, and intensity are different training variables.

They must not be treated as interchangeable.

The agent should evaluate all three when designing and adjusting resistance training.

---

# 1. Training Volume

Training volume describes the amount of training performed.

There are multiple ways to measure volume.

For practical hypertrophy programming, the agent should primarily track:

- Hard sets per muscle group
- Repetitions
- Resistance/load
- Exercise difficulty
- Weekly training frequency

Total tonnage can sometimes be useful, but it is not a perfect measure of training stimulus, especially for bodyweight and resistance-band exercises.

---

# 2. Hard Sets

A hard set is a set performed sufficiently close to failure to provide meaningful training stimulus.

For practical programming, sets around approximately 1–3 RIR can generally be considered productive working sets.

Very easy warm-up sets should not automatically be counted as equivalent to hard working sets.

The agent should distinguish:

- Warm-up sets
- Technique practice
- Productive working sets
- Failure/testing sets

---

# 3. More Volume Is Not Always Better

Increasing volume can increase training stimulus, but it also increases fatigue and recovery requirements.

The relationship is not:

more sets = more progress forever

At some point, additional volume may provide diminishing returns or become counterproductive.

The agent should increase volume only when there is a clear reason.

Possible reasons include:

- Progress has stalled despite adequate recovery.
- A muscle is receiving insufficient stimulus.
- The current workload is well tolerated.
- The user's schedule allows additional training.
- Additional work is compatible with running and recovery.

---

# 4. Volume Landmarks

The agent should not blindly prescribe a fixed number of sets as universally optimal.

Useful concepts include:

### Minimum Effective Volume

The smallest amount of training that produces meaningful progress.

### Maximum Recoverable Volume

An approximate upper boundary beyond which additional training becomes difficult to recover from.

These are individual and can change over time.

The agent should learn the user's practical training tolerance from logged data.

---

# 5. Frequency

Training frequency describes how often a muscle, movement, or training modality is performed.

For resistance training, frequency should be chosen based on:

- Total weekly volume
- Recovery
- Exercise selection
- Session length
- User schedule
- Training priority

The same weekly volume can often be distributed across different numbers of sessions.

---

# 6. Current Default Frequency

The user's current default structure is:

- Strength: 3 sessions/week
- Running: 3 sessions/week

This is a starting architecture, not a permanent requirement.

The agent may modify frequency when necessary.

---

# 7. Frequency and Recovery

Higher frequency is not automatically better.

Increasing frequency can help distribute volume, but it can also increase scheduling complexity and repeated fatigue.

The agent should consider whether additional frequency actually improves training quality.

If the user can make good progress with fewer sessions, unnecessary frequency should not be added.

---

# 8. Intensity

The word "intensity" can mean different things in training.

For resistance training, intensity is often used to describe load relative to maximum strength.

For example:

- 80% of 1RM
- 70% of 1RM

This is different from effort.

---

# 9. Intensity vs Effort

These concepts must not be confused.

### Intensity

How heavy the resistance is relative to the user's maximum capability.

### Effort

How close the set is to failure.

RIR is primarily an effort measure.

For example:

A light resistance exercise taken to 1 RIR can have:

- Low relative load intensity
- High effort

A heavy set stopped far from failure can have:

- High load intensity
- Lower effort

The agent should use the correct concept when making decisions.

---

# 10. Repetition Range and Intensity

Hypertrophy can occur across a broad range of repetitions when the set is sufficiently challenging.

Therefore, the agent should not assume:

8–12 reps = hypertrophy

and:

1–5 reps = strength only

There is substantial overlap.

However, different repetition ranges can have different practical advantages.

Lower repetitions may be useful for:

- Strength development
- Heavy compound movements

Moderate repetitions may be useful for:

- General strength and hypertrophy

Higher repetitions may be useful for:

- Isolation work
- Bodyweight movements
- Exercises where heavy loading is impractical

Exercise selection and fatigue must be considered.

---

# 11. Volume and Running

The user's training includes both resistance training and running.

Total training stress is therefore not simply resistance-training volume.

The agent should consider:

Resistance-training volume
+
Running volume
+
Running intensity
+
Recovery capacity

For example, adding several lower-body sets during a week with increased running volume may create excessive fatigue even if the strength volume appears reasonable in isolation.

---

# 12. Lower-Body Training

Lower-body volume requires particular attention because running already provides substantial repeated loading.

The agent should monitor:

- Squat/lunge volume
- Hip-hinge volume
- Calf volume
- Running distance
- Running intensity
- Leg soreness
- Performance
- Joint/tendon discomfort

Lower-body training should not be increased automatically just because upper-body training is progressing.

---

# 13. Upper-Body Training

Running generally places less direct muscular stress on the upper body than lower-body training.

Therefore, upper-body volume can often be progressed more independently.

However, overall recovery still matters.

The agent should consider:

- Sleep
- Nutrition
- Total fatigue
- Session duration
- Exercise overlap

---

# 14. Volume Distribution

Weekly volume can be distributed across multiple sessions.

For example, instead of:

12 hard sets for a muscle in one session

the system could use:

6 sets + 6 sets

if that improves:

- Set quality
- Recovery
- Performance
- Session length

The agent should prioritize useful distribution over arbitrary frequency targets.

---

# 15. When to Add Volume

Consider adding volume when:

- The user is consistently recovering.
- Performance is stable or improving.
- Current volume has been maintained long enough to evaluate.
- The target muscle may benefit from additional work.
- There is no major competing fatigue.
- The user has sufficient time.

Add volume gradually rather than making large jumps.

---

# 16. When to Reduce Volume

Consider reducing volume when:

- Performance repeatedly declines.
- Fatigue accumulates.
- Recovery worsens.
- Soreness persists unusually long.
- Joint/tendon discomfort increases.
- Running performance deteriorates.
- Motivation falls substantially.
- Training quality declines.

Reducing volume does not mean the program failed.

It can be an appropriate adjustment to restore recoverability.

---

# 17. Volume During a Deload

A deload generally reduces training stress.

Volume is often one of the easiest variables to reduce.

The agent may:

- Reduce sets
- Maintain some movement practice
- Reduce exercise difficulty
- Increase RIR
- Reduce running duration or intensity

The exact adjustment should depend on the source and severity of fatigue.

---

# 18. Volume Progression

The agent should not increase:

- Sets
- Repetitions
- Resistance
- Running volume

all at the same time without a reason.

Changing multiple stress variables simultaneously makes it difficult to determine what caused improvements or fatigue.

Prefer controlled progression.

---

# 19. Training Density

Training density describes how much work is performed within a given amount of time.

Increasing density can increase training stress even when sets and repetitions remain unchanged.

Examples:

- Shorter rest periods
- More work in the same session duration

Density should therefore be treated as another fatigue variable.

It should not be manipulated casually during a hypertrophy-focused program.

---

# 20. Rest Periods

Rest periods should be long enough to maintain useful performance.

Short rest is not automatically better.

For demanding compound movements, longer rest may be necessary.

For smaller movements, shorter rest may be sufficient.

The agent should prioritize:

- Performance
- Technique
- Target muscle stimulus
- Session practicality

rather than forcing a universal rest duration.

---

# 21. Measuring Volume for Bands and Bodyweight

Traditional tonnage is less useful when resistance is difficult to quantify.

For these exercises, prioritize:

- Sets
- Repetitions
- RIR
- Exercise variation
- Band/setup
- Range of motion
- Technique

This creates a more useful longitudinal record.

---

# 22. Decision Rules

### If volume is increasing + performance improving + recovery good

Continue gradual progression.

### If volume is increasing + performance declining

Do not automatically add more volume.

Investigate fatigue and recovery.

### If volume is stable + performance improving

Maintain the volume and allow progression through resistance, repetitions, or exercise difficulty.

### If volume is stable + performance stagnant

Investigate:

- Recovery
- Nutrition
- Technique
- RIR accuracy
- Exercise selection
- Running load
- Training consistency

before increasing volume.

### If fatigue is high

Reduce training stress rather than adding more work.

### If the user has a major increase in running load

Consider temporarily maintaining or reducing lower-body resistance volume.

---

# 23. Core Principle

Volume, frequency, and intensity are tools.

The agent should not optimize one variable in isolation.

The objective is:

**Enough training stimulus to create adaptation, distributed intelligently across the week, while keeping total training stress recoverable.**
