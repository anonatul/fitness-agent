# Recovery

## Purpose

Recovery is the process that allows the body to respond to training and
adapt to the training stimulus.

Training creates stress.

Recovery determines whether the user can successfully adapt to that
stress and perform future training.

The agent must therefore treat recovery as a core training variable,
not an optional consideration.

---

# 1. Training and Recovery

The basic process is:

Training stimulus
→ Fatigue
→ Recovery
→ Adaptation

The objective is not to eliminate fatigue.

Some fatigue is expected.

The objective is to keep training stress within a level that the user
can recover from consistently.

---

# 2. Main Recovery Factors

Important recovery factors include:

- Sleep
- Nutrition
- Hydration
- Training load
- Rest between sessions
- Stress
- Daily activity
- Illness
- Pain or injury
- Overall lifestyle

The agent should consider multiple factors rather than relying on a
single recovery measurement.

---

# 3. Sleep

Sleep is one of the most important recovery variables.

A practical target for the user is approximately:

**7–9 hours per night**

The agent should consider:

- Sleep duration
- Sleep quality
- Recent sleep consistency
- Whether poor sleep is temporary or persistent

One bad night's sleep does not automatically require cancelling a
workout.

Repeated poor sleep should influence training decisions.

---

# 4. Nutrition

Adequate nutrition supports:

- Training performance
- Muscle recovery
- Glycogen replenishment
- Muscle protein synthesis
- General health

Important factors include:

- Adequate total calories
- Sufficient protein
- Sufficient carbohydrates
- Adequate dietary fat
- Micronutrients
- Hydration

Aggressive calorie restriction can reduce recovery capacity.

---

# 5. Hydration

Hydration affects exercise performance, especially during running and
hot conditions.

The agent should encourage appropriate fluid intake based on:

- Weather
- Sweat rate
- Session duration
- Exercise intensity
- Individual needs

Long or particularly sweaty sessions may require consideration of
electrolyte replacement.

---

# 6. Recovery Is Individual

Two people performing the same workout may recover differently.

Recovery capacity depends on:

- Training history
- Fitness
- Sleep
- Nutrition
- Stress
- Age
- Life demands
- Training volume
- Training intensity
- Concurrent training

The agent should therefore learn from the user's own response rather
than assuming a universal recovery timeline.

---

# 7. Soreness

Muscle soreness is not a reliable standalone measure of recovery.

Soreness can occur without productive training.

A lack of soreness does not mean that training was ineffective.

The agent should evaluate soreness together with:

- Performance
- Energy
- Sleep
- Fatigue
- Joint/tendon discomfort
- Recent training load

---

# 8. Performance as a Recovery Signal

Training performance is one of the most useful practical indicators.

Examples of possible warning signs:

- Normal loads suddenly feel much harder.
- Repetitions decrease repeatedly.
- Running pace becomes unusually difficult.
- Normal easy running feels unusually hard.
- Technique deteriorates earlier than expected.

One poor session is not enough to conclude that recovery is inadequate.

Repeated performance decline is more meaningful.

---

# 9. Energy

The user can report subjective energy.

A simple scale can be:

- 1 = extremely low
- 2 = low
- 3 = normal
- 4 = good
- 5 = very good

Energy should be treated as one input rather than an absolute decision
rule.

---

# 10. Fatigue

Fatigue can be physical, neurological, psychological, or systemic.

Useful signs include:

- Persistent tiredness
- Reduced motivation
- Declining performance
- Poor sleep
- Heavy legs
- Increased perceived effort
- Reduced training quality

The agent should look for combinations of warning signals.

---

# 11. Resting Heart Rate

Resting heart rate can provide useful information about recovery when
tracked consistently.

The agent should primarily look at trends rather than individual
readings.

A temporary increase can result from:

- Poor sleep
- Stress
- Dehydration
- Illness
- Training fatigue
- Caffeine
- Measurement conditions

Therefore, a single elevated reading should not automatically trigger a
major training change.

---

# 12. Heart Rate and Running

During running, heart rate can help evaluate:

- Aerobic effort
- Recovery
- Training intensity
- Changes in fitness

However, heart rate is affected by:

- Temperature
- Hydration
- Stress
- Sleep
- Caffeine
- Terrain
- Pace
- Sensor accuracy

The agent should interpret heart rate alongside pace and perceived effort.

---

# 13. RPE

RPE means **Rate of Perceived Exertion**.

It describes how difficult an effort feels.

A simple 1–10 scale can be used:

- 1–2 = extremely easy
- 3 = easy
- 4 = moderate
- 5 = somewhat hard
- 6 = moderately hard
- 7 = hard
- 8 = very hard
- 9 = extremely hard
- 10 = maximal effort

RPE is particularly useful for running because pace alone does not
capture how difficult the session actually felt.

---

# 14. Easy Running and Recovery

Easy running should generally remain easy enough to recover from.

Useful indicators include:

- Conversational breathing
- Controlled effort
- Reasonable pace
- Low-to-moderate RPE
- Ability to complete the run without excessive fatigue

The agent should not turn every run into a performance test.

---

# 15. Recovery Between Strength Sessions

Muscles and connective tissues may recover at different rates.

The agent should consider the previous session before training the same
muscles hard again.

If a muscle is still significantly fatigued, the agent may:

- Reduce volume
- Reduce effort
- Change exercise
- Shift the session
- Maintain the session if symptoms are minor and performance is good

The decision should depend on the overall state, not soreness alone.

---

# 16. Concurrent Training Recovery

The user performs both strength training and running.

Therefore, recovery must be evaluated across the entire system.

Example:

A user may feel recovered from an upper-body workout but still have
significant lower-body fatigue from running.

The agent should distinguish local fatigue from whole-system fatigue.

---

# 17. Hard Sessions

Hard sessions create greater recovery demands.

Examples:

- High-effort lower-body strength sessions
- Hard intervals
- Long runs
- High-volume workouts

Hard sessions should not be stacked unnecessarily.

The weekly schedule should provide enough recovery to maintain training
quality.

---

# 18. Recovery and Scheduling

The agent should consider session interaction.

For example:

Hard lower-body strength
→ next-day hard running

may be less desirable than:

Hard lower-body strength
→ easier/recovery day
→ quality running

The exact schedule depends on the user's current training load and
goals.

---

# 19. Recovery During Poor Sleep

If sleep is poor for one night but the user otherwise feels normal,
training may continue with caution.

If poor sleep becomes persistent:

- Reduce training stress if needed.
- Avoid unnecessary high-intensity work.
- Prioritize recovery.
- Monitor performance.

The agent should not automatically cancel every workout after one poor
night.

---

# 20. Recovery During Illness

Training should be modified when the user is sick.

The agent should prioritize recovery rather than maintaining the planned
training load.

If symptoms are significant, worsening, or concerning, appropriate
medical evaluation should take priority.

The agent must not diagnose illness.

---

# 21. Pain Is Not Normal Fatigue

The agent must distinguish:

### Normal training fatigue

- Muscle tiredness
- Temporary heaviness
- Expected post-training soreness

from:

### Potential injury warning signs

- Sharp pain
- Persistent joint pain
- Worsening tendon pain
- Pain that changes movement
- Pain that progressively worsens during exercise
- Significant swelling
- Loss of normal function

Pain should not be treated as evidence of an effective workout.

---

# 22. Recovery Score

A simple internal recovery state can combine:

- Sleep
- Energy
- Soreness
- Fatigue
- Performance
- Resting HR trend
- Pain/injury flags

The agent should not reduce recovery to one arbitrary numerical score
unless the score is useful for the application.

Raw data should remain available.

---

# 23. Recovery Categories

The system can classify the user's current state approximately as:

### Green

Recovery is good.

Normal training and progression can be considered.

### Yellow

Some recovery concerns exist.

Training can continue with adjustments such as:

- Reduced volume
- Slightly higher RIR
- Easier running
- Fewer hard sets

### Red

Significant warning signals exist.

Normal training should not automatically continue.

Potential actions:

- Reduce or stop the relevant training
- Prioritize recovery
- Avoid high-intensity work
- Assess pain/injury concerns
- Seek professional medical guidance when appropriate

These categories should support decision-making rather than replace
judgment.

---

# 24. Recovery Trends

The agent should prioritize trends over isolated observations.

Example:

One bad workout:

**Low concern**

Three consecutive poor workouts + worsening sleep + high fatigue:

**High concern**

The system should recognize accumulation.

---

# 25. Recovery and Deload Decisions

A deload or reduced-training period may be appropriate when multiple
signals indicate accumulated fatigue.

Potential combination:

- Performance declining
- Sleep poor
- Energy low
- Persistent soreness
- Running feels harder
- Motivation declining

The agent should consider the minimum adjustment necessary to restore
training quality.

---

# 26. Recovery Decision Rules

### If performance is improving + recovery is good:

Continue normal progression.

### If performance is stable + recovery is good:

Maintain or gradually progress.

### If performance is slightly worse + recovery is otherwise good:

Do not immediately change the program.

Monitor the next sessions.

### If performance is declining repeatedly + fatigue is high:

Reduce training stress and investigate the cause.

### If pain is increasing:

Modify or stop the relevant exercise and assess appropriately.

### If running suddenly feels much harder:

Check sleep, hydration, illness, heat, fatigue, and recent training load
before assuming loss of fitness.

### If recovery is poor across several days:

Prioritize recovery over progression.

---

# 27. Core Principle

Recovery is not the absence of training.

Recovery is the process that allows training to produce adaptation.

The agent should continuously balance:

**Training stimulus ↔ Recovery capacity**

The best program is not the hardest program the user can survive.

It is the hardest useful program the user can consistently recover from
and progress on.
